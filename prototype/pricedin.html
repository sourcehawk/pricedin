    How to judge these inputs

    Every result on this page follows from these inputs. These rules of thumb help you pick values you can defend.

    1. Desired annual return. Set the return that makes the risk worth it to you. A broad index fund of US stocks (the S&P 500) has returned about 10% a year over long periods, with large swings along the way. One stock carries more risk than an index, so many investors ask for more, often 12% to 15%. A higher target makes more stocks look expensive. A lower one accepts returns you could get from an index with less effort.
    2. Holding period. Use how long you would really hold. Over 1 or 2 years, the exit P/E decides most of the result, and that depends on market mood. Over 3 to 5 years, earnings growth and the P/E both matter. Over 10 years, growth dominates, but a 10-year growth forecast is much less certain.
    3. Exit P/E: when a company usually gets a high P/E. Investors pay a high P/E when they expect earnings to grow fast for many years. Common reasons: steady, repeat revenue such as subscriptions, high profit margins, little need for new factories or equipment, a strong position against competitors, and low interest rates. A P/E can also look high for a bad reason: earnings dropped for a while, for example at the bottom of a business cycle, so the price is divided by an unusually small number.
    4. Exit P/E: when a company usually gets a low P/E. Slow or shrinking growth, heavy debt, earnings that swing with the economy, businesses that need a lot of new investment to grow, and high interest rates all push the P/E down. Be careful with cyclical companies near a peak: the P/E looks low because earnings are unusually high, and those earnings may fall.
    5. Exit P/E: what to pick. As growth slows, the P/E usually falls. That is why the Estimated setting lowers today's P/E as growth fades. Assume a higher exit P/E than today only with a clear reason. Compare with the stock's own typical P/E (set next to Typical P/E in the exit P/E lever, 36 months by default) and with the P/E bands in the price chart. For reference, the S&P 500 has averaged roughly 16× earnings over its long history and has often traded near 20× or higher in recent decades.
    6. Growth baseline. Weighted, the default, follows recent execution but smooths out single quarters. Today reacts first when growth changes, but one unusual quarter can move it. Trailing gives a plain growth rate over the number of months you choose: a long period such as 36 months is steady but can miss a slowdown that already started. Use Custom when you know something the record does not show, such as a product cycle ending or a one-time boost. Year by year lets you model a slowdown, for example 15%, 12%, 9%. Few companies grow EPS above 15% to 20% a year for a long time. Growth usually fades toward the growth of the whole economy, which is around 4% to 6% a year before inflation is removed.
    7. Dividends. Enter the yearly dividend per share if the company pays one. Leave it at 0 to judge the price return only.

    Watch out. These are rules of thumb, not laws. Interest rates, the industry and the whole market change what counts as a normal P/E. Test a few values on each lever and see whether the verdict changes.

    How to read the priced-in summary

    This panel answers one question: how fast must the company grow its earnings for you to get the return you want, and has it grown that fast before?

    1. The big number is the bar to clear. It is the average yearly growth in earnings per share (EPS) that the company needs, so that you earn your desired return. It assumes you sell at the exit P/E: the price-to-earnings ratio you expect the market to pay when you sell.
    2. What it takes in dollars. Under the big number, the same requirement in dollars: the share price you need when you sell, and the EPS the company must reach so that your exit P/E gives that price.
    3. Compare it with what the company has done. There are three reference points, the same as the growth baseline options in the left panel. The weighted trend averages the last 8 quarters of year-over-year growth, with the latest quarters counting most. Today uses only the latest quarter. Trailing growth is the average yearly growth over the months you set next to Trailing growth in the left panel (12 by default). On the scale, the large dot is the requirement. Any growth rate in the green part of the scale beats it. A smaller dot to the left of the large dot falls short.
    4. Read the gap. The gap is your chosen baseline minus the requirement, in percentage points (pp). A small negative gap means the company must speed up a little. A large negative gap means the price already assumes a much better future than the company's record shows.
    5. Read the verdict label. The colored label under the big number sums up the gap in one word. Undemanding your growth baseline beats the requirement by more than 1.5 points a year, so the company already grows fast enough.Tight the baseline is within 1.5 points either way, so you reach your target only if growth holds.Demanding the baseline falls short by more than 1.5 points, so the price assumes growth will speed up.The label follows the baseline you pick in the left panel.
    6. Check the direction with the quarterly chips. Each chip compares one quarter's EPS with the same quarter a year earlier. If the numbers fall quarter after quarter, growth is slowing, and the weighted trend will keep dropping. If they rise, growth is speeding up.
    7. Move the levers to test how fragile the result is. Try the two levers marked in the left panel and watch the big number. A higher desired return or a lower exit P/E raises the requirement. A longer holding period makes the exit P/E matter less each year. If a small lever change flips the verdict color, the investment has little room for error.

    Watch out. EPS can jump for reasons that do not repeat, such as a tax benefit, an asset sale or large share buybacks. Before you trust a growth rate, check in Financials that revenue and operating income grew too.

    Price

    Demo: daily prices are simulated between the profile's quarter-end closes. The live version plots real daily closes and steps the bands on each filing date.

    How to read the price chart

    The chart shows whether the share price moved because the company earned more, or because investors became willing to pay more (or less) for the same earnings.

    1. The price line. The share price at each day's close. It is the only line with an axis, so read its value on the left.
    2. The TTM EPS line. Earnings per share over the last 12 months: the last four quarterly reports added up. It moves in steps, one each time a new report comes out. Like every line except the price, it is stretched to fill the chart, so compare its shape, not its height.
    3. The P/E line. The price divided by TTM EPS: how many dollars investors pay for one dollar of yearly earnings. When it rises, the stock got more expensive compared with its earnings.
    4. The 12-month change lines. How much the price and TTM EPS changed compared with one year earlier. These two share one scale, so you can compare their heights. They are hidden by default: switch them on with their chips above the chart.
    5. Compare the shapes of the price and TTM EPS lines. If they climb together, the price follows earnings. If the price climbs while the EPS line stays flat, investors pay more for the same earnings.
    6. Confirm with the P/E line. A rising P/E means the price grew faster than earnings, so the stock got more expensive. A falling P/E means earnings grew faster than the price, so the stock got cheaper, even if the price went up.
    7. Use the P/E bands for levels. Each dotted line shows what the share price would be if investors paid one fixed P/E, labeled on the right, such as 20×. It is TTM EPS times that number, so the line steps up each time earnings rise. Read where the price sits among the lines: that is its P/E at that moment, and the shaded band is where it sits today. If the price stays with one line, it rose only because earnings rose. If it climbs from a lower line to a higher one, investors started paying more for each dollar of earnings.
    8. Check the share change markers. A red marker means the company issued new shares that quarter, a green one means it bought shares back. After a red marker, EPS grows slower than total profit, because the profit is split over more shares.
    9. Compare the two 12-month lines. They are hidden by default, so turn them on with their chips when you want to see where price and earnings went separate ways. They share one scale, so you can compare their heights. When price change sits above EPS growth, the P/E went up over that year. When it sits below, the P/E went down. The faint line marked 0% (12M) is where there was no change.
    10. Understand the correlation number. The number sits under the chart title, as “price–earnings link” with its value. Correlation measures how closely two lines move together, on a scale from −1 to +1. Here it compares the two 12-month lines: when earnings growth sped up or slowed down, did the price change follow? Strong, 0.6 or more: earnings news moves this stock.Moderate, 0.3 to 0.6: earnings explain part of the moves.Weak, below 0.3: other things moved the price, such as market mood, interest rates or hopes for the future.Negative: the price tended to move against earnings growth.

    Watch out. The lines other than the price are stretched to fill the chart, so compare their direction and timing, not their heights. The two 12-month lines are the exception. In a 6M or 1Y range, EPS changes only a few times, so the correlation rests on few reports. Treat it as a hint, not proof.

    How to read the return sensitivity grid

    The grid shows what you would earn under many combinations of earnings growth and the P/E you sell at, so you can see how much each one matters.

    1. Rows are EPS growth rates. Each row is one average yearly EPS growth rate over your holding period of 3 years, read from the label on the left. The highlighted row is one example.
    2. Columns are exit P/E multiples. Each column is one P/E at which you could sell at the end of your holding period, 3 years from now, read from the label at the top. The highlighted column is your exit P/E, shown in purple at the top.
    3. Where they meet is your return. The cell where a row and a column cross is the yearly return from today's price for that growth rate and that exit P/E. The boxed cell is the return for the example row at your exit P/E. Hover over any cell in the grid, or tap it, to see the calculation.
    4. YOU marks your own case. The cell marked YOU sits in the row for your growth baseline and the column for your exit P/E. It is the return you get if your own assumptions come true.
    5. Red cells fall short of your target. In a red cell, that growth rate and exit P/E give you more than 1.5 points a year less than your desired return. Red does not mean a loss: a red cell can still be a gain, just smaller than you asked for. The deeper the red, the bigger the shortfall.
    6. Green cells beat your target. In a green cell, you earn more than 1.5 points a year above your desired return. The deeper the green, the more room for error. Look at where green starts in your column: if it only starts well below your row, the stock needs faster growth than your baseline.
    7. Follow the amber cells. Amber cells are combinations that land within 1.5 points of your target. Cells on the red side fall short, cells on the green side beat it. A wide green area around your cell gives a margin of safety.
    8. Question your cell's color. The color of your YOU cell rests on two assumptions, marked in the left panel: your growth baseline and your exit P/E. Check how far each one is from changing the color. If a small change flips it, the result is fragile.
    9. Move across a row to see what the P/E does. The growth stays the same, only the selling P/E changes. If the cells turn red quickly to the left, your return depends on the market keeping a high P/E.
    10. Move down a column to see what growth does. The P/E stays the same, only growth changes. The row where the color turns green is roughly the growth the price requires at that P/E.
    11. Req marks the required growth. The required growth (Req) row is the growth rate closest to what today's price requires for your target at your exit P/E. In your column, rows above it fall short of your target and rows below it beat it.
    12. Wtd marks the weighted trend. Weighted (Wtd), Today and Trailing (Trl) are the growth baseline options from the left panel, placed on the grid so you can compare them. The weighted trend (Wtd) row is closest to the company's year-over-year EPS growth over the last 8 quarters, with the latest quarters counting most. If it sits above the required growth (Req) row, the current pace falls short of what the price needs.
    13. Today marks the latest quarter. The Today row is closest to the EPS growth of the latest quarter compared with the same quarter a year earlier. If it sits above the weighted trend (Wtd) row, growth has been slowing lately.
    14. Trl marks trailing growth. The trailing growth (Trl) row is closest to the average yearly growth of TTM EPS over the months set next to Trailing growth in the left panel (12 by default). A longer period gives a steadier number, so compare it with the weighted trend (Wtd) and Today to see whether growth is speeding up or slowing down.

    Watch out. Colors compare each return with your target, not with zero, so a red cell can still be a gain. Growth is an average: a bad year followed by strong recovery can land in the same cell as steady growth, but it is harder to hold through.

    How to read the earnings trajectory

    1. The reported EPS line. The solid line is TTM EPS as the company reported it: earnings per share over each past 12 months, one point per quarter. It ends at today.
    2. The baseline line. The grey dashed line continues from today at your growth baseline from the left panel. It shows where EPS ends up if the company keeps growing the way you assume.
    3. The required line. The blue dashed line is the EPS path that today's price needs for your target return at your exit P/E. Its end point is the EPS the company must reach by the time you sell.
    4. Compare the slopes. Look at how steep the reported line was over the last year, then at the required path. If the required path is much steeper, the price assumes faster growth than the company has shown recently.
    5. Read the shading. The shaded area is the gap between the two dashed lines.The company grows too slowly for your target, so you would earn less than you want.The company grows fast enough, so you would earn more than you want.The wider the gap at the right edge, the bigger the difference.
    6. Question the color. The color is only as good as your two assumptions, marked in the left panel. A big green area can come from an exit P/E set too high or a growth baseline that is too optimistic. It can also mean the market sees a risk you do not, such as a slowdown or earnings at a peak. A big red area can come from an exit P/E set too low or a baseline dragged down by one weak quarter. It can also mean the market expects an acceleration, or earnings that are only temporarily low. Either way, ask why the market disagrees with you.
    7. Check for bends in the history. A reported line that flattens near today signals slowing growth, even if the longer trailing growth still looks strong.

    Watch out. Only the end point of each path matters for the return. The smooth curves are one way to get there. Real earnings arrive in uneven steps.

    How to read how demanding the stock has been

    1. The required growth line. Each point is the yearly EPS growth you would have needed if you had bought on that date, using today's levers: your target return, holding period and exit P/E.
    2. The delivered growth line. Each point is how much TTM EPS actually grew over the year before that date. It is what the business had just shown when you would have bought.
    3. Follow the required line over time. When it falls, the stock got easier to own, because earnings grew faster than the price. When it rises, the price ran ahead of earnings.
    4. Compare the two lines. When the required line sits above the delivered line, the price asked for more than the business had just delivered. When it sits below, the business was growing faster than the price required.
    5. Compare today with the past. If today's required point is the highest on the chart, the stock is more demanding now than at any time in the range.

    Watch out. A lower requirement does not make a stock cheap by itself. Earnings can fall later, and each point uses only the earnings known on that date.

    Bear, base, bull

    Your own three cases over the same holding period. No probabilities are assigned.

    How to set up bear, base and bull

    Three cases help you see a range of outcomes instead of one guess. Bear is a bad outcome, base is the expected one and bull is a good one.

    1. The bear case. A bad but plausible outcome: growth slows further and the P/E drops, for example toward the lower P/E bands in the price chart. By default it uses 6 points less growth than the base case and a 25% lower P/E. Type your own numbers into the card.
    2. The base case. What you expect: by default the weighted trend and your exit P/E. Change it to your own view of the company.
    3. The bull case. A good outcome: growth picks up and investors pay more. By default it uses 6 points more growth than the base case and a 20% higher P/E.
    4. Read the three together. If only the bull case beats your target, the stock needs things to go well. If even the bear case comes close, the price leaves room for mistakes.
    5. Size the downside. Look at the bear case's total return. That is the loss to plan for if things go badly.

    Watch out. No probabilities are assigned. The three cases do not say which one is likely, so weigh them with what you learn about the business.

    How to read the financials

    Earnings per share is the end result. These numbers show where it came from and whether it is likely to last.

    1. Start with revenue. Revenue (sales) is the hardest number to inflate. If EPS grows much faster than revenue for a long time, the difference must come from higher margins, share buybacks or one-off items.
    2. Check operating income and its margin. Operating income is the profit from the core business. The operating margin is that profit as a share of revenue. A rising margin means each dollar of sales brings more profit. That can last, but margins cannot rise forever.
    3. Compare operating costs with revenue. If costs grow faster than revenue for several quarters, the margin shrinks and profit growth slows, even while sales grow.
    4. Watch the share count. When a company issues new shares, for example to pay employees in stock or to buy another company, each existing share owns less of the profit. That is dilution. A rising share count makes EPS grow slower than profit, and a falling one (buybacks) makes it grow faster. The price chart marks quarters where the share count changed by 2% or more.
    5. Compare free cash flow with earnings. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash the business brings in, minus what it spends on equipment and buildings. If it stays well below earnings for several quarters, the earnings are less reliable than they look.
    6. Compare each quarter with a year earlier. Many businesses are seasonal, so each YoY label compares a quarter with the same quarter one year earlier.
    7. Use Compare to see metrics side by side. Since the first quarter shows each metric's total change over the period, so you can see which grew fastest. Each quarter shows the step from one quarter to the next. For example, EPS rising above operating income means something other than the business, such as buybacks or lower taxes, lifted EPS. The table below the chart shows which metrics moved together from quarter to quarter.
    8. Tie it back to the price. If revenue and operating income support the growth the price requires, the priced-in number is believable. If only EPS does, look deeper before you trust it.

    Watch out. Single quarters move with timing, such as large orders or tax payments. Look at several quarters before you draw a conclusion.
